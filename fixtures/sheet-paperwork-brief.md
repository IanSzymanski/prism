## At a glance

::: stats
- **74.9%** of child welfare supervisors name stress and burnout as a top-three reason caseworkers leave[^opre]
- **r = 0.69** correlation between administrative workload and emotional exhaustion[^pazer]
- **30–200%** of a worker's annual salary is the cost of one caseworker departure[^lushin]
:::

Burnout in social work is severe, it is costly, and it shows up in every country that has measured it. A 2025 systematic review covered 21 studies and more than 24,000 social workers across five continents. It found the same risk factors everywhere: workload, work–family conflict and lack of support.[^chaves]

## Four findings

### 1. The *kind* of work predicts turnover more than the amount

::: cols
**Caseload size** was not a significant predictor of turnover intention in a meta-analysis of U.S. child welfare research.[^kim]

**Emotional exhaustion** was, at r = 0.59 with intent to leave. That is a large effect in this literature.
:::

Hiring is necessary but not sufficient. If the work itself is exhausting, adding people spreads the exhaustion across more of them.

### 2. Administrative work is the strongest driver of exhaustion

A 2025 study of 143 German social workers isolated documentation and compliance as their own variable. Administrative workload was the strongest predictor of exhaustion in the model. Public-sector workers were more exhausted than nonprofit peers carrying the same workload, which the author attributes to stricter templates, audits and less discretion (Pazer, 2025).

### 3. It takes up close to half the week

Observational research in the UK found social workers spend **39–45%** of their time on administrative work.[^ferguson] A comparable figure of about 40% is cited for Germany.

### 4. The people leaving are the people carrying it

Fewer than half of surveyed U.S. caseworkers intended to stay five years. The study's own recommendation was to reduce caseload *and administrative paperwork* (Lushin et al., 2023). National child welfare turnover averages roughly 30%, against a healthy human-services benchmark of about 12%.

## Two levers

| Lever | What it includes | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Build resources** | Supervision, peer support, wellness and resilience programs | Buffers the effect of demands, but doesn't remove them |
| **Reduce demands** | Documentation, compliance, re-entry of information | Removes exhaustion at its source |

: Simplified from the job demands–resources model used across this research.

> Leaning on resilience risks shifting the blame for burnout onto the professionals themselves.
>
> [Paraphrasing Pazer, 2025]{.cite}

Most agencies have spent a decade on the first lever. The demand side has barely moved, because documentation is mandated and the tools haven't changed.

::: {.callout .tint}
#### []{.icon .ph-sparkle .accent} A signal from healthcare

When clinicians in six health systems used ambient AI documentation, self-reported burnout fell from **51.9% to 38.8%** within 30 days.[^olson] Those were physicians, not case managers, and it hasn't been replicated in social work yet. It is still the strongest evidence so far that cutting documentation time cuts burnout, not just hours.
:::

## What this means for agency leaders

::: checks
- Measure documentation time locally. There is no U.S. federal time-and-motion figure, so your own number is the one that counts.
- Treat paperwork as a workforce issue, not only an efficiency issue.
- Keep investing in support, and add demand reduction alongside it.
- Judge tools by whether the documentation gets smaller, not only faster to type.
:::

::: callout
#### []{.icon .ph-file-text} Reading the evidence carefully

The Pazer study covers one German state, cross-sectional, with short scales; read it as the study that isolated the administrative variable, not as definitive. Kim and Kao's caseload finding concerns turnover intention, not service quality or safety. U.S. federal data measures perceptions and turnover reasons, not documentation hours.
:::
